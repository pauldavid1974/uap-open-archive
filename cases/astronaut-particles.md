# Astronaut "fireflies" and particles

**Status: explained** for the Mercury fireflies NASA's catalog entry already explains. Not every particle comment in these files carries that same sentence. Read the file, not the nickname.

## In plain language

Early astronauts saw bright flecks outside the window. They named some of them "fireflies," after John Glenn. In the PURSUE catalog, NASA's description of Gordon Cooper's Mercury-Atlas 9 flight says NASA later determined those fireflies were frozen condensation coming off the spacecraft, lit by the sun. Wally Schirra, a flight earlier, said the flecks seemed to come from his own capsule and looked like metal shavings. Scott Carpenter compared others to snowflakes. The catalog does not, in Carpenter's entry, repeat the frozen-condensation conclusion.

## What the official catalog says

**Label: official.**

- [NASA-UAP-D010](../records/nasa-uap-d010.md), Mercury-Atlas 9, May 15, 1963. Cooper notes "John's fireflies." "NASA later determined that the fireflies are attributable to frozen condensation separating from the spacecraft body."
- [NASA-UAP-D011](../records/nasa-uap-d011.md), same flight. Cooper also describes brilliant white particles drifting from the spacecraft, and fireflies after deploying beacons that are spherical equipment with xenon strobes. Those beacons are identified equipment, not a mystery, in this description.
- [NASA-UAP-D012](../records/nasa-uap-d012.md), Schirra, October 3, 1962. Particles from the capsule itself. See [his case](schirra-mercury-atlas-8.md) for the separate "tag-along" claim, which this description does not make.
- [NASA-UAP-D013](../records/nasa-uap-d013.md), Scott Carpenter, Mercury-Atlas 7, May 24, 1962. White particles that "look exactly like snowflakes," some seeming to move faster than the spacecraft. This description stops there.

## What not to do with this

**Label: analysis.** These files are often pulled into later stories about escorts or unknowns. Where the catalog already gives an ordinary account, that account is the official one on the page. It can still be wrong, but an argument against it has to say why, and it has to be labeled. This archive did not listen to the audio.
